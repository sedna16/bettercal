Learn about how Caloocan grew from a tiny farming barrio at the northern edge of Manila into the sprawling, history-steeped metropolis it is today — from its role in the birth of the Philippine Revolution to its transformation into one of the most populous cities in the country.

## Origins of the Name

There are two folk explanations for how Caloocan got its name. Some say it comes from the Tagalog word _**lo-ok**_ (bay), as the early settlement stood near the waters of Manila Bay and the lake of Dagat-Dagatan. Others say it came from _**sulok**_ (corner), because the town once sat "in the corner" where the old towns of Tondo and Tambobong (Malabon) met. Either way, the name is a play on words such as _**kasulok-sulokan**_ or _**kaloob-looban**_, which mean "innermost area" — a fitting description for a settlement tucked inland from the coast.

Before it was known as Caloocan, the settlement was a small barrio of Tondo that the Spanish called **Libis Espina** or **Aromahan**, a name linked to the lowland (_libis_) where it was located.

## Spanish Era: From Barrio to Municipality

Augustinian missionaries first reached the settlement in **1762**, and by **1765** they had established the first Catholic church in the area, the **San Roque Church**. In **1814**, spiritual administration of the parish was handed over to the Recollect order.

The decisive step came in **1815**, when Caloocan was separated from Tondo and became an independent municipality. Its original territory was enormous by today's standards — it stretched to the foothills of Marikina, San Mateo, and Montalban in the east; the Tinajeros, Tanza, and Tala rivers in the north; San Francisco del Monte, Sampaloc, Sta. Cruz, and Tondo in the south; and Dagat-Dagatan in the west.

## The Philippine Revolution and the Katipunan

Caloocan's place in Philippine history is defined by the **Revolution of 1896**. The town was the center of activity for the **Katipunan**, the secret society that launched the fight for independence from Spain. **Andrés Bonifacio**, the Supremo, and his followers held meetings in Caloocan, and the town's outskirts became the stage for some of the revolution's **first armed encounters** with Spanish forces in August 1896.

The gathering known as the **Cry of Balintawak** (or Pugad Lawin) — where Katipuneros tore up their _cedulas_ (poll tax receipts) as a gesture of defiance — took place in this area, and **Melchora Aquino**, better remembered as **Tandang Sora**, the "Mother of the Katipunan," sheltered and fed the revolutionaries from her home nearby. **Gregoria de Jesús**, founder and vice-president of the women's chapter of the Katipunan and wife of Bonifacio, also came from a prominent Caloocan family.

## American Rule and Territorial Changes

Under the American administration, Caloocan became one of the towns of the Province of **Rizal** in **1901**. In **1920**, the adjoining territory of **Novaliches** was consolidated into Caloocan.

The second half of the American period saw Caloocan shrink. After **Commonwealth Act No. 502 (1939)** created **Quezon City**, Caloocan ceded the barrios of Balingasa, Kaingin, Kangkong, La Loma, Malamig, Matalahib, Masambong, San Isidro, San Jose, Santol, and Tatalon. Then, under the **Republic Act No. 333 (1948)** boundary settlement, it lost Baesa, Talipapa, San Bartolome, Pasong Tamo, Banlat, Kabuyao, Pugad Lawin, Bagbag, Pasong Putik, and the old Novaliches poblacion. The remainder of the old Novaliches territory became what is now **North Caloocan** — which is why the city has two separate territories, North and South, split by Quezon City.

## World War II and the Liberation

Like the rest of Manila, Caloocan was occupied by Japanese forces during **World War II**. During the **Battle for Manila in 1945**, the northern approaches to the city — in and around Caloocan, including the La Loma and North Cemetery areas — saw heavy fighting as Filipino and American forces drove out the occupying army.

## Cityhood and Metro Manila

After years as a growing municipality, Caloocan was converted into a city under the **Charter of the City of Caloocan (Republic Act No. 3278)**, approved by Congress in 1961. In a plebiscite held on **February 16, 1962**, the people of Caloocan ratified the charter, officially making it a city.

Caloocan remained a city of the province of Rizal until **November 7, 1975**, when **Presidential Decree No. 824** created the National Capital Region — better known as **Metro Manila** — and Caloocan became one of its constituent cities.

## Growth and Development

From a modest string of barrios, Caloocan has become one of the most populous cities in the Philippines. The **2020 census** recorded **1,661,584** residents, and the city's own figures now report a population of about **1.8 million**. It is also one of the largest local government units in Metro Manila by **land area**, covering roughly 55 square kilometers.

Caloocan's development has been driven by its position as a transport and industry hub. The **Monumento** area, where EDSA, the MacArthur Highway, Rizal Avenue Extension, and Samson Road converge, is a major gateway to northern Luzon — served by the LRT-1, the PNR line, and the North Luzon Expressway. Industrial zones, commercial districts, and residential communities have grown rapidly on both the North and South sides, making the city a vibrant economic center of the metro.

## Heritage and Culture

Caloocan's greatest heritage landmark is the **Bonifacio Monument** (popularly called **Monumento**), a 13.7-meter bronze-and-granite sculpture at the center of the Monumento roundabout. Designed by National Artist **Guillermo Tolentino** and unveiled on **November 30, 1933**, it commemorates Andrés Bonifacio and the Katipunan and was declared a **National Cultural Treasure** in 2009.

Other heritage sites include the historic **La Loma Cemetery**, one of the oldest burial grounds in the country, and sites associated with the Katipunan era. The city also observes its anniversary, **Araw ng Caloocan**, every **February 16**, and honors Bonifacio Day with activities at the Monumento on **November 30**. Blending revolutionary history, hard-working industry, and lively urban culture, Caloocan carries its past proudly into the present.

## Key Dates at a Glance

- **1762** – Augustinian missionaries arrive; **1765** San Roque Church is established.
- **1815** – Caloocan is separated from Tondo and becomes an independent municipality.
- **1896** – Caloocan becomes a center of the Katipunan; the Philippine Revolution begins in its vicinity.
- **1901** – Caloocan becomes a town of the Province of Rizal.
- **1939** – Commonwealth Act No. 502 transfers several barrios to the new Quezon City.
- **1945** – Caloocan sees heavy fighting during the Battle for Manila.
- **1948** – Republic Act No. 333 redefines the boundary with Quezon City; several barrios are transferred out, shaping today's North–South division.
- **1962** – Residents ratify the Caloocan City Charter (Republic Act No. 3278) on February 16.
- **1975** – Presidential Decree No. 824 makes Caloocan part of Metro Manila.
- **2009** – The Bonifacio Monument is declared a National Cultural Treasure.
